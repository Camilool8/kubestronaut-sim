An exam simulator you run yourself.

A timed rehearsal for the Kubernetes certifications, on a real cluster on your own machine. Deliberately harder than the real exams.

View the repository Documentation

Three commands

Everything after up happens in the browser. A countdown that cannot be paused. A real Linux desktop with terminals, on a real cluster built for the exam you picked. A per-check score with full solutions afterwards, and a record of every attempt to measure the next one against.

Choose your operating system

Requires Docker Desktop on the WSL2 backend and about 9GB of free RAM. The PowerShell launcher reads JSON natively, so python3 is not needed here.

Requires Docker Desktop, python3, and about 9GB of free RAM.

The XFCE desktop is inside that memory figure. Nothing is built until you pick an exam.

The numbers behind it

  • 141

    Questions written

    44 hands-on for CKAD and 97 multiple-choice for KCNA, counted from the spec.questions list in each bank's exam.yaml file. All original: no third-party exam dump may be committed here, because CC BY-SA 4.0 requires the banks to be ours to license.

  • 2 of 5

    Certifications with a bank

    The other three are shown in the catalog with the reason they cannot be sat, rather than as five working exams. A bank that cannot produce a meaningful score is not offered at all.

  • 0 / 100%

    The two grading gates

    A fresh environment must score 0 and the reference solutions must score 100%. tests/smoke.sh asserts both against a real stack, and CONTRIBUTING.md says why CI cannot run it and when it has to be run by hand.

The certification track

One bank is loaded at a time, because a bank is a Kubernetes cluster seeded for its questions — so choosing another one is a destructive 2-4 minute rebuild, and the app asks first. Your attempt record sits on its own volume and survives it.

Why the score means something

Hands-on questions are graded on behaviour wherever behaviour is the point — a policy that actually denies, an Ingress the controller really routes — not on the shape of your YAML. That is only possible because the environment is complete enough to test an answer the way the real exam expects.

Calico, not kindnet
NetworkPolicies are genuinely enforced, so a policy question can be graded on whether traffic is actually denied.
ingress-nginx
An Ingress question has a controller behind it, so the route either resolves or it does not.
A local Helm repository and registry
Plain HTTP, on the cluster network, so the image-building and packaging half of the curriculum is answerable offline.
Weights the build checks, and the grader applies
A domain's share of an exam's points is its published curriculum weight, and tests/bank-weights.sh fails the build if any domain drifts more than two points from it.

Three ways to run an attempt

Offered gentlest first. Being recorded is one of these permissions, not a separate setting: a Mastery or Exam run is kept as an attempt, a Training run is not.

What each mode permits, compared across Training, Mastery and Exam
Training Mastery Exam
Clock None. Untimed. Half the bank's duration 60 min CKAD, 45 min KCNA The bank's full duration 120 min CKAD, 90 min KCNA
Help Two-tier hints, and solutions on demand None None
Scoring Does not end the attempt Ends the attempt Ends the attempt
Kept as an attempt No Yes Yes

The timer is server-side, not in the tab. In Exam mode it cannot be paused: at zero the exam ends and grading starts by itself. Training is the untimed opt-out, which is how this meets WCAG 2.2.1.

Mode is chosen when the attempt starts and cannot change after that. The three are defined in the API reference, down to the wire ids and the gate each one closes.

Two ways to run it

Both of them are things you run. There is no service here to sign up for and no account to make: the local product is the reference, and the hosted shape is the same simulator with a door in front of it. If you want the uncapped one, clone this and run it.

One hub is reachable from the internet and does identity, seats, the queue and history. Each candidate gets the whole ./sim up stack as a single Pod, and the facilitator inside it has no authentication and gains none: it is simply never reachable except through the hub. That is what lets the local product stay untouched.

A hands-on session runs a privileged container

It builds a real Kubernetes cluster inside itself and hands a stranger a root shell next to it. Deploy it only on nodes you are willing to rebuild. A NetworkPolicy that walls a session off from the cluster hosting it ships on by default.

The hosting guide covers seats, sizing, credentials and what hosted mode does differently. Multiple-choice sessions are unprivileged and tiny.

What this is not

Stated rather than implied, because the alternative is letting a reader infer something the software does not do.

Not a replacement for the rest of your preparation.
It does not replace working a problem out for yourself, the lab you build at home, or the Linux Foundation's own training. What it adds is a timed rehearsal on a real cluster and a score you can trust.
No accounts, and no authentication anywhere.
A password field on a single-user tool you cloned yourself would be theatre. The only real control is which interface it binds to, so it binds loopback by default and nothing off your machine can reach it. Opting into your LAN is one setting, and not one your host firewall covers — SECURITY.md sets out that whole boundary.
Hosting it is your job, not ours.
There is no service to sign up for. The Helm chart that puts it in front of a group is something you deploy, on your hardware, under your own identity provider. Both shapes are set out above.
No pass-rate claims, and no percentile.
A score here measures practice progress; it does not predict a real exam result. Your record describes your own progress and never a population's, so nothing compares you to other candidates.
No third-party exam dumps.
Every question is original. The banks are CC BY-SA 4.0, which requires them to be ours to license.
No hands-on exam on a phone.
The answer surface is a real remote desktop, so small screens get an explanation instead of a broken layout. A KCNA attempt does work on a phone.
