An exam simulator you run yourself.

A timed rehearsal for the Kubernetes certifications, on a real cluster on your own machine. Deliberately harder than the real exams.

View the repository Documentation

Three commands

Everything after up happens in the browser: a countdown that cannot be paused, a real Linux desktop with terminals, a real Kubernetes cluster built for the exam you picked, a per-check score with full solutions afterwards, and a record of every graded attempt to measure the next one against.

Requires Docker Desktop (or docker + compose v2), python3, and about 9GB of free RAM — the XFCE desktop is inside that figure. Nothing is built until you pick an exam: choosing one in the browser is what creates its cluster, and the page shows that build's progress step by step.

Two ways to run it

Both of them are things you run. There is no service here to sign up for and no account to make: the local product is the reference, and the hosted shape is the same simulator with a door in front of it. If you want the uncapped one, clone this and run it.

One hub is reachable from the internet and does identity, seats, the queue and history. Each candidate gets the whole ./sim up stack as a single Pod, and the facilitator inside it has no authentication and gains none: it is simply never reachable except through the hub. That is what lets the local product stay untouched.

What a seat costs

Hands-on session
3.9Gi / 540m requested, 11.8Gi / 4400m limit Summed across its eight containers, measured on a two-node cluster.
Multiple-choice session
128Mi, and it schedules anywhere No cluster is involved, so this pool costs almost nothing. Setting the hands-on seats to zero hosts only these.
Node disk
32.9GiB of ceilings per session 20GiB of that is the inner Docker daemon. A ceiling rather than a reservation, but three seats on one node can legitimately ask for 99GiB.
Boot
1 Pod creation in flight at a time Boot is CPU-bound: one measured session took a four-core node to 3090m. Two boots on one node do not take turns, they both crawl.

A hands-on session runs a privileged container

It builds a real Kubernetes cluster inside itself with a container runtime and hands a stranger a root shell next to it, and there is no way to do that unprivileged. Treat those nodes as disposable: deploy on hardware you are willing to rebuild and keep them out of any pool running something you care about. The chart ships a NetworkPolicy that stops a session reaching the cluster hosting it, on by default, and turning it off is not a supported configuration — it is the boundary that makes this an accepted risk rather than an open one. Multiple-choice sessions are unprivileged and tiny, and none of this applies to them.

The full hosting guide covers seats, sizing, credentials, and the four things hosted mode does differently.

The numbers behind it

  • 123

    Questions written

    26 hands-on for CKAD and 97 multiple-choice for KCNA, counted from the spec.questions list in each bank's exam.yaml file. All original: no third-party exam dump may be committed here, because CC BY-SA 4.0 requires the banks to be ours to license.

  • 2 of 5

    Certifications with a bank

    The other three are shown in the catalog with the reason they cannot be sat, rather than as five working exams. A bank that cannot produce a meaningful score is not offered at all.

  • 0 / 100%

    The two grading gates

    A fresh environment must score 0 and the reference solutions must score 100%. tests/smoke.sh asserts both against a real stack; CI cannot run it, so it is run by hand for any change that could affect grading.

The certification track

One bank is loaded at a time, because a bank is a Kubernetes cluster seeded for its questions — so choosing another one is a destructive 2-4 minute rebuild, and the app asks first. Your attempt record sits on its own volume and survives it.

Three ways to run an attempt

Offered gentlest first. Being recorded is one of these permissions, not a separate setting: a Mastery or Exam run is kept as an attempt, a Training run is not.

What each mode permits, compared across Training, Mastery and Exam
Training Mastery Exam
Clock None. Untimed. Half the bank's duration 60 min CKAD, 45 min KCNA The bank's full duration 120 min CKAD, 90 min KCNA
Help Two-tier hints, and solutions on demand None None
Scoring Does not end the attempt Ends the attempt Ends the attempt
Kept as an attempt No Yes Yes

Training is how the product meets WCAG 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable. The unpausable countdown stays the default, but it is opted into rather than imposed.

The timer is server-side, not in the tab, and in Exam mode it cannot be paused. At zero the exam ends and grading starts by itself.

What this is not

Stated rather than implied, because the alternative is letting a reader infer something the software does not do.

Not a replacement for the rest of your preparation.
This is one part of getting ready, not the whole of it. It does not replace working a problem out for yourself, the lab you build at home, or the Linux Foundation's own training. What it adds is a timed rehearsal on a real cluster and a score you can trust.
No accounts, and no authentication anywhere.
In the simulator, permanently. What you clone is a local single-user tool and a password field would be theatre; the only real control is which interface it binds to. Ports bind to all interfaces by default, so on a network you do not control, start it with SIM_BIND=127.0.0.1 ./sim up instead.
Hosting it is your job, not ours.
There is no service here to sign up for. Running this needs nothing but Docker on your own machine, and the Helm chart that puts it in front of a group is something you deploy, on your hardware, under your own identity provider. Nobody operates a shared instance of this for you. Both shapes are set out above, including the part where a hands-on session is a privileged container.
No adoption numbers, testimonials, or pass-rate claims.
There are none to report. A score here measures practice progress; it does not predict a real exam result.
No benchmark, and no percentile.
Graded attempts are kept — best score per exam, weakest domains, progress along the five-certification path — but that record is one candidate's. It can describe your progress and never a population's, so nothing here compares you to other candidates. Training runs are not recorded at all: practice with the solutions open would make every best score meaningless.
No third-party exam dumps.
Every question is original. The banks are CC BY-SA 4.0, which requires them to be ours to license, so a dump could never be committed here.
No hands-on exam on a phone.
The desktop requirement is functional, not a styling shortcut: the answer surface is a real remote desktop. Small screens get an explanation instead of a broken layout. A KCNA attempt does work on a phone, because the multiple-choice engine never touches the cluster.

Why the score means something

Hands-on questions are graded on behaviour wherever behaviour is the point — a policy that actually denies, an Ingress the controller really routes — not on the shape of your YAML. That is only possible because the environment is complete enough to test an answer the way the real exam expects.

Calico, not kindnet
NetworkPolicies are genuinely enforced, so a policy question can be graded on whether traffic is actually denied.
ingress-nginx
An Ingress question has a controller behind it, so the route either resolves or it does not.
A local Helm repository and registry
Plain HTTP, on the cluster network, so the image-building and packaging half of the curriculum is answerable offline.
Weights the build checks, and the grader applies
A domain's share of an exam's points is its published curriculum weight, and tests/bank-weights.sh fails the build if any domain drifts more than two percentage points from it. That gate certifies the authored bank, not a draw taken from one, so the grader weights the score directly as well, and reports the raw points beside it.
